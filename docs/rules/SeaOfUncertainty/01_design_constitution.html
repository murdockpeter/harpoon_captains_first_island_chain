Design Constitution • v0.1

Design Constitution

The rules that govern the rules. Every mechanic must serve the same game: approachable operational naval warfare with high agency, meaningful risk/reward, and uncertainty that reflects player decisions rather than replacing them.

1. Agency Before Procedure

Every major phase must present at least one meaningful player choice. If a phase exists only to maintain the simulation, merge it, simplify it, or automate it.

2. Detail Must Create Decisions

Keep detail when it changes player behavior. Remove detail that exists only to prove fidelity. Range, signature, endurance, sensor posture, weapon commitment, and readiness matter because they alter choices. Decorative sub-ratings do not.

3. Every Strong Option Carries Cost

Design test: If an option is powerful and almost always correct, it is probably missing a price.
ChoiceRewardRisk / Cost
High-Tempo movementArrive soonerHigher signature, endurance use, friction
Active searchBetter detectionGreater exposure
Strike nowSeize tempoWorse targeting / weaker coordination
SynchronizeOverwhelm reactionDelay, stale intel, coordination risk
Push through frictionPreserve timingCommand strain
RecoverRestore effectivenessSurrender tempo

4. Luck Reflects Decisions

Players should construct the likely result range before a die is rolled. Randomness determines the exact expression of uncertainty inside that range. The dice do not decide whether the plan mattered.

A player should say, “I accepted a risky shot with stale targeting and it failed,” not “the dice decided the game.”

5. Physical Warfare Remains Tangible

The theory may shape the game, but it may never erase the game. Players still maneuver formations, establish patrol lines, manage sensor posture, launch strikes, defend against incoming weapons, expend ammunition, take damage, and fight for geographic objectives.

6. Time Is a Resource

Actions consume operational time. Friction primarily changes time. Waiting can improve preparation but surrender initiative. Synchronization is valuable because effects arriving inside the opponent’s reaction window behave differently from the same effects arriving separately.

7. Information Is Imperfect, Stale, and Contestable

Detection does not permanently reveal the enemy. Contacts possess location certainty, identity certainty, and age. Search improves knowledge but can expose the searcher. Deception succeeds when it causes the opponent to move, wait, expend attention, or fire at the wrong problem.

8. Command Attention Is Finite

Routine execution should not consume command attention. Changing missions, forcing timing, focusing intelligence, and coordinating complex operations should. Command is represented as limited attention and staff capacity, not a generic mana pool.

9. Entropy Is a Core System — But Never a Generic Morale Track

Entropy represents the portion of a formation’s physical potential that is temporarily unavailable for effective use because friction, disruption, destruction, exhaustion, uncertainty, and command problems interfere with execution.

Entropy must always have visible causes. A player should be able to look at a formation and understand why it is losing effectiveness.

Entropy SourceTypical CauseLikely Consequence
FrictionComplex activity, high tempo, difficult conditionsDelay / added action time
DisruptionEW, cyber, deception, command attackCommand and information impairment
DestructionCombat losses and physical damageReduced capability and increased disorder
ExhaustionSustained operations / low enduranceHigher friction and slower recovery
Mission ChurnRepeated retasking and interventionLost cohesion and planning efficiency

The player should not normally “remove an entropy point.” Instead, the player addresses causes: recover, simplify the mission, restore communications, replenish, slow the tempo, or accept the disorder and keep pressing.

10. Cohesion Is the Visible Summary of Entropy

Formation Cohesion is the concise player-facing summary of accumulated entropy. Use a small state ladder such as Cohesive / Disrupted / Disorganized, but ensure that each state was produced by concrete events rather than arbitrary marker accumulation.

Relationship: Physical Capability = what the formation could theoretically do. Entropy = how much of that capability is currently unavailable. Cohesion = the visible summary of that operational condition.

11. One Mechanical Language

Every rule should alter one or more of these core variables:

Time

When can this force act?

Position

Where is it, and what can it influence?

Information

What does the player think is true?

Command

What can headquarters change or coordinate?

Entropy / Cohesion

How much capability is unavailable, and how effectively can the formation execute?

Physical Capability

What can the force actually do?

12. The Phase Sequence Teaches the Game

A new player should be able to follow the phase descriptions and understand what choices are available without memorizing the entire rulebook. Each phase should include a short “Ask yourself” prompt and a rules reference.

13. Layers of Mastery

First game: move, search, shoot, defend. Fifth game: signatures, readiness, endurance, reserves, and weapon conservation. Fifteenth game: tempo, deception, command workload, synchronization, and manipulating the enemy’s picture. Same rules — deeper understanding.

14. Operational Skill, Not Stat Optimization

The best player should win primarily by managing uncertainty, position, attention, timing, delegation, risk, and synchronization better than the opponent — while still understanding the capabilities of the forces at sea.

Rule-Adoption Test

  1. Does the player make a choice?
  2. Are there genuine alternatives?
  3. Is there tangible risk and reward?
  4. Does the rule interact with the existing system?
  5. Does it increase meaningful play more than bookkeeping?
Default verdict: a rule that fails two of these tests does not enter the game.
